Not everyone wants full, fast masculinization. Some nonbinary and genderqueer people (and some trans men) want slower changes, fewer changes, or a short course of T to "bank" certain permanent effects and then stop. All of those are valid goals. This page covers what low-dose testosterone is, what it realistically can and can't do.

## You have the right to an individualized plan

WPATH SOC8 Chapter 8 (Nonbinary) is explicit: clinicians should "provide nonbinary people with individualized assessment and treatment that affirms their experience of gender," and should consider hormones and surgery "in the absence of 'social gender transition.'"[^1] You don't have to want a binary transition, a standard dose, or every effect, to be offered care. Good clinicians will ask about your **embodiment goals** such as which specific changes, to which specific body parts, would be affirming rather than "masculinization" as a package deal.[^2]

## What "low-dose" actually means

There's no agreed definition of "microdosing" as it's a community term, not a clinical one.[^2] In practice, low-dose regimens look like UCSF's "Initial - low dose" tier in its [masculinizing therapy guideline](https://transcare.ucsf.edu/guidelines/masculinizing-therapy), which the guideline itself describes as "recommended for genderqueer and nonbinary dosing":[^3]

- **Injections:** ~20 mg/week testosterone cypionate or enanthate (the two common injectable forms of T) (vs 50-100 mg/week standard)
- **Gel:** 12.5-25 mg/day (vs 50-100 mg/day standard)

Some people deliberately maintain testosterone levels in an *intermediate* range between typical female and male ranges.[^3] The usual strategy is "start low, titrate slowly to effect". titrating just means adjusting the dose gradually, guided by your goals, your levels, and how your body responds.[^3]

## The evidence is nearly nonexistent

Be skeptical of anyone who claims certainty here, in either direction:

- The only cohort study (a study following a group of people over time) of low-dose T in nonbinary people (Nolan et al., ENDO 2024, n=46 using transdermal T, meaning gel or patches, median level ~11 nmol/L) found 87% were still on low-dose at ~14 months, 13% escalated to full dose, and 3 people (7%) developed polycythemia (too many red blood cells). **Physical outcomes were not measured at all** so we don't actually know how much slower or lesser changes were.[^4]
- **No randomized trial of low-dose testosterone has ever been completed.** A randomized trial (RCT) is the gold-standard study type, where people are randomly assigned a regimen. The first one (measuring dysphoria, euphoria, and mental health) is still recruiting at [Austin Health, Melbourne](https://www.transresearch.org.au/participate/lowdose).[^5] A small retrospective audit (looking back at clinic records) (Ward-Smith 2026) and one youth review round out the literature; an NHS England evidence review found zero eligible studies for nonbinary partial masculinization in under-18s.[^6]
- Everything else is expert extrapolation (careful guesswork) from binary-transition and cis-puberty data. Even the 2024 expert review by Dimakopoulou & Seal states plainly that regimens "have not been examined by randomised controlled clinical trials."[^7]

## What low-dose does and doesn't do

**It slows changes down.** That's the one consistent expectation. the same menu of effects, arriving more gradually (and possibly to a lesser final degree but "slower vs lesser" is unresolved until trials report).[^4][^7]

**It does NOT reliably spare your voice.** This is the most important myth to kill. The larynx responds at *moderate* testosterone levels, avoiding voice change would require keeping levels very low, **below ~7 nmol/L**, which is barely above typical pre-T levels.[^7] Voice F0 (pitch) drops don't track serum (blood) androgen levels, and community reports confirm even microdoses can permanently deepen the voice.[^8] If preserving your voice is a priority, the strategies experts discuss are *limiting duration* (stopping before the voice "breaks," usually 6-12 months in) or simply not taking T not low doses.[^7] See [[Voice Training]] for non-hormonal options.

**It usually won't stop your periods.** Lower doses frequently don't suppress menstruation so if stopping bleeding is a goal, you'll likely need an add-on (see below).[^7][^9]

**You cannot pick and choose effects.** At any dose. SOC8: "if facial hair development is needed, genital growth is inevitable" and the timeframe that produces hair changes usually brings an irreversible voice drop too.[^1] UCSF: "it is not possible to prospectively choose a regimen that will predictably allow patients to arrive at a specified configuration of sex characteristics."[^3] Bottom growth and libido (sex drive) increase are near-universal; breast/chest tissue is not meaningfully reduced by T at any dose.[^7]

## Tools people combine with (or use instead of) low-dose T

- **Finasteride/dutasteride** (blockers of DHT, a stronger hormone made from testosterone): proposed to keep voice + muscle changes while reducing facial/body hair and scalp hair loss. The trade-off cuts both ways: DHT also drives **bottom growth**, and SOC8 warns 5α-reductase inhibitors "may impair clitoral growth and the development of facial and body hair."[^1] Higher doses risk "regression of some virilization" and sexual side effects (UCSF).[^3] Net effect on overall virilization (masculinization) in trans men is unknown. [^10]
- **Menstrual suppression without T:** progestins (progesterone-like hormones such as norethindrone/norethisterone or medroxyprogesterone), GnRH agonists (medications that switch off the ovaries' hormone signals), a levonorgestrel IUD (a hormone-releasing device placed in the uterus), or the implant are standard options, used with or without T. Irregular bleeding is common in the first months; long-term DMPA (the 3-monthly injection) carries a bone-density caution.[^9][^11] (Note: norethisterone is sometimes avoided because it can aromatize to ethinylestradiol, a type of estrogen.[^7])
- **Minoxidil for facial hair:** one trans-specific case report + one 16-person cohort + one cis RCT. see [[FTM Community Knowledge]] for more information.

## Stopping, pausing, and time-limited courses

If you stop T and still have your ovaries, SOC8 Chapter 8 describes what reverts and what persists:[^1]

| Persists (permanent) | Reverts toward baseline |
|---|---|
| Voice deepening | Periods return (usually 3-6 months) |
| Facial/body hair follicles | Fat redistribution |
| Bottom growth | Muscle mass & strength |
| Male-pattern scalp hair loss | Skin oiliness/acne, libido shifts, body-hair *rate* |

A **planned, continuous, time-limited course** (e.g., 6-12 months) to acquire irreversible changes and then stop is a clinically recognized strategy, UCSF describes it explicitly, but it is lacking in evidence, and the effects you get before stopping are luck of the genetic draw.[^3][^2]
## Monitoring on a low dose

SOC8 notes the implications of long-term low-dose therapy are undocumented and advises considering cardiovascular (heart and blood vessel) and **bone health** monitoring. sex hormones protect bone, and intermediate levels sit below the usual protective range.[^1] Expert reviewers suggest DEXA scanning (a low-radiation scan that measures bone density) every 3-5 years plus lipid checks (blood fats like cholesterol) for people maintained below male-range levels.[^7] Hematocrit (your red-blood-cell level) still applies (7% polycythemia in the Nolan cohort even at low dose).[^4] See [[FTM Blood Testing]].
