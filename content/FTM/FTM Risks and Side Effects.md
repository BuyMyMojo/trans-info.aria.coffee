Testosterone at cismale levels has been well-studied. This page sizes each risk against the evidence and links you to what to do about it. For dosing and level targets see [[FTM Dosages]] and [[FTM Target Ranges]]; for monitoring logistics see [[FTM Blood Testing]].

## The risk table

## Erythrocytosis

Testosterone stimulates red-blood-cell production. Too many red blood cells thickens the blood, that's erythrocytosis, and it's the main thing your blood tests watch for. In the largest cohort (a group of people followed over time, 1,073 trans men, 20-year follow-up), **11% exceeded hematocrit 0.50** (hematocrit = the share of your blood made up of red cells) at some point, 3.7% exceeded 0.52, and 0.5% exceeded 0.54 and risk keeps accumulating (10% probability by year 1, 38% by year 10).[^1] This is why WPATH SOC8 (Statement 12.17)[^2] asks for hematocrit/hemoglobin monitoring for transmasculine-specific lab monitoring.

**What raises your risk**: smoking (OR \~2.2), long-acting undecanoate injections (OR \~2.9), higher BMI (OR \~3.7), and older age at start.[^1] (OR = odds ratio, how much each factor multiplies your risk.) Injectable forms of raised hematocrit more than gel in the ENIGI cohort.[^3]

**What to do about it**: check T levels (including peak), lower the dose, shorten the injection interval or switch to transdermal (gel or patches), quit smoking, lose weight if relevant, treat any sleep apnea, and rule out other causes; phlebotomy (having blood taken out) or blood donation is a short-term bridge.[^4] UCSF's [plain-language overview of masculinizing therapy](https://transcare.ucsf.edu/guidelines/masculinizing-therapy) walks through the same actions.

> [!warning] The threshold when this becomes an issue isn't known
> The Endocrine Society flags Hct >50% as "very high risk," while the >54% "pause T" threshold is imported from cis-men TRT (testosterone replacement therapy) guidance, Madsen 2021 explicitly notes it's unknown whether cis-male limits apply to trans men.\[[^1]\]\[[^5]\] Follow your prescriber's threshold; no validated trans-specific number exists (see [[FTM Evidence Gaps]]).

## Skin and hair

**Acne** is one of the most common early effects (up to \~81% at 6 months in one cohort), peaks around 6 months/first year, then declines, 94% have no or only mild acne long-term.[^12][^13] Treat it like any acne. gentle skincare, over-the-counter topicals first, then prescriptions via your GP or dermatology. Keeping levels in range and avoiding big injection-interval peaks helps.[^4][^14]

**Scalp hair loss** is genetically determined and common long-term: \~33% mild frontotemporal loss (receding at the temples) and \~31% moderate-severe androgenetic alopecia (male-pattern hair loss) at \~10 years.[^12]

> [!warning] Finasteride has a real trade-off
> Finasteride and dutasteride block DHT (dihydrotestosterone, testosterone's stronger byproduct), and DHT drives some of the changes you may want. WPATH SOC8, verbatim: "treatment with a 5α-reductase inhibitor may impair clitoral growth and the development of facial and body hair in trans masculine individuals."[^2] UCSF adds that the 5 mg dose or dutasteride "may result in excessive testosterone blockade, and resultant sexual side effects and regression of some virilization."[^4] If you're early in transition and want bottom growth and facial hair, discuss timing carefully. Minoxidil (topical or oral) doesn't interfere with T and is the lower-stakes option along with microneedling/PRP.[^15]

## Pelvic pain

Pelvic pain on T is common around \~23% of trans adolescents in one cohort, more in community surveys, and it was **community knowledge years before the clinical literature caught up** (see [[FTM Community Knowledge]]).[^18] Causes are mixed and often unclear. atrophic tissue changes (tissue getting thinner and drier), cramping that tracks injection cycles, breakthrough bleeding, pelvic floor issues. Interestingly, it's associated with slightly *lower* T levels and breakthrough bleeding so incomplete suppression may contribute.[^19] Workup (checking for causes) first; treatments used include topical vaginal estrogen, lubricants, progestins or a hormonal IUD, switching to a smoother regimen, and pelvic floor physio.[^20] UCSF has a [dedicated guideline page on pelvic pain in trans men](https://transcare.ucsf.edu/guidelines/pain-transmen) worth bringing to your clinician.

## Myth corrections

- **"T damages your liver."** False for modern therapy. The reputation comes from oral 17-alkylated methyltestosterone (an old chemical form of T), a bodybuilding-era drug no longer used. Endocrine Society: "serious hepatic toxicity is not anticipated with parenteral or transdermal testosterone use" (that is, injected or through-the-skin T).[^5][^4] Transient liver-enzyme bumps on blood tests occasionally happen and usually resolve on their own.
- **"T causes 'roid rage.'"** Not at therapeutic (medical) doses. The classic randomized controlled trial (Pope 2000) gave cis men up to 600 mg/week which is **6-12× typical transmasc dosing** and even then 84% had minimal psychiatric effects.[^21] In trans men, systematic review evidence shows improved quality of life, less depression and anxiety; aggression doesn't increase beyond early adjustment, and T level doesn't track anger.[^22][^23] Short-term irritability in the first months is common for some (you are having effectively another puberty) and is usually fixed by smoothing peaks such as switching to gel or a shorter injection interval.[^4] See [[FTM Myths and FAQ]].

## Drug interactions

- **Warfarin/anticoagulants (blood thinners):** androgens change anticoagulant activity, more frequent INR (a blood-clotting measure) monitoring when starting or changing dose.
- **Insulin/oral hypoglycemics (diabetes pills):** androgens can lower blood glucose so insulin needs may drop. Watch for low blood sugar episodes.
- **Corticosteroids (steroid anti-inflammatories like prednisone):** increased fluid retention, caution with heart, kidney, or liver disease.
- **HIV antiretrovirals (HIV medications):** NNRTIs (one class of HIV meds) may lower T levels; ritonavir/cobicistat may raise them, mention all meds to your prescriber or check with a trusted medical professional.[^25]
- **Finasteride/dutasteride:** no chemical interaction, but it blunts DHT-driven masculinization (see the trade-off above).
